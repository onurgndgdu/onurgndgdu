### PERDE 1 — KANCA · 0:00–0:22

> In 1750, a man walked through the streets of London carrying an object
> you probably own three of. [...]
>
> People stopped to stare. Strangers shouted at him. Children followed
> him down the street. Coachmen tried to splash him with mud. //
>
> He kept doing it for the next thirty years.
>
> This is the story of the umbrella — and the man England humiliated
> for refusing to get wet.

---

### PERDE 2 — LONDRA, 1750 · 0:22–2:00

> To understand why a piece of waxed cloth on a stick could ruin a man's
> reputation, you have to understand what London was. [...]
>
> It rained. Constantly. And the streets had no drainage worth the name.
> Rain turned the roads into a slurry of mud, horse dung, ash, and
> whatever the houses had thrown out that morning. //
>
> And the men walking through it were dressed for a drawing room.
> Wool coats. Silk waistcoats. Powdered wigs that cost more than a
> servant earned in a year — and dissolved in water. //
>
> So when the rain came, a gentleman had three options.
>
> He could stay indoors and miss his business.
> He could walk, and arrive ruined.
> Or he could pay for a hackney coach. [...]
>
> Now — umbrellas already existed. They had existed for thousands of
> years. But not for this. //
>
> The word itself gives it away. *Umbrella* comes from the Latin
> *umbra* — shade. It was a sun device. In the ancient world it shaded
> emperors and priests. In 18th-century Europe it shaded women's
> complexions on bright days. [...]
>
> A parasol was an accessory. For ladies.
>
> Which meant that in 1750, there was no such thing as a man's umbrella.
> There was only a woman's parasol — being carried by a man.

---

### PERDE 3 — HANWAY · 2:00–3:45

> Jonas Hanway was not a man you would expect to start a fight with
> an entire city. //
>
> Born in 1712. A merchant. He had spent years abroad — trading through
> Russia, travelling as far as Persia, keeping careful notes the whole
> way. He came home to London in his late thirties with money, opinions,
> and a habit of writing both of them down. [...]
>
> He had also spent time in France. And in France, he had seen something
> useful. //
>
> Sometime in the early 1750s, Jonas Hanway walked out into a London
> downpour holding an umbrella. [...]
>
> He is widely remembered as the first Englishman to do it. That claim
> is a tradition rather than a documented fact — but whether or not he
> was first, he was certainly the most visible. And the most mocked. //
>
> The reaction was immediate. Onlookers hooted and jeered. Street
> children trailed after him making noise. According to several accounts,
> people threw rubbish at him.
>
> And the coachmen — the coachmen were the worst. [...]
>
> Because for them, this wasn't about fashion. //
>
> Rain was their harvest. Every downpour filled their coaches with
> gentlemen who would rather pay than be ruined. A man who could walk
> through a storm and arrive dry was a man who would never hire them
> again. [...]
>
> So they drove close to the kerb. They aimed for the puddles.
> They tried to make the umbrella not worth carrying.

---

### PERDE 4 — NEDEN UTANÇ VERİCİYDİ · 3:45–5:30

> But the coachmen were only defending their wages. The crowd's contempt
> came from somewhere deeper. [...]
>
> There were two charges against the umbrella, and Hanway was guilty
> of both.
>
> The first: it was effeminate. //
>
> Parasols belonged to women. A man holding one had, in the logic of
> the street, picked up a woman's object and announced that he was too
> delicate for weather. [...]
>
> And English masculinity in this period was built on exactly the
> opposite idea. A man endured. He stood in the rain and did not
> complain about it. Hardiness wasn't a preference — it was a virtue,
> and visibly avoiding discomfort was a visible admission that you
> lacked it. //
>
> The second charge was worse. The umbrella was French. [...]
>
> Hanway had brought the habit back from the continent, and everyone
> could tell. In the 1750s, Britain and France were sliding toward war —
> the Seven Years' War would break out in 1756. Anything French was
> suspect: French manners, French tailoring, French softness. [...]
>
> So the man with the umbrella wasn't just vain. He was vain *in a
> foreign way.* //
>
> There is a particular irony here, and Hanway would have hated it
> being pointed out. In 1756, he published an essay attacking tea —
> arguing it weakened the nation and corrupted the working poor.
>
> The man London called soft and foreign spent his spare time warning
> England that it was going soft on a foreign import. [...]
>
> He simply drew the line in a different place than everyone else did.

---

### PERDE 5 — OTUZ YIL · 5:30–7:00

> Here is the part of the story that actually matters. //
>
> Hanway did not stop.
>
> He didn't write a defence. He didn't campaign. He didn't try to
> convince anyone of anything. He just kept walking out into the rain,
> for roughly thirty years, holding the thing that made people laugh
> at him. [...]
>
> And he was not a man who could be dismissed as a crank. //
>
> In 1756 he helped found the Marine Society, a charity that equipped
> poor boys and men for service at sea — one of the more effective
> philanthropic operations of the century. He campaigned on behalf of
> abandoned children. He was, by the standards of his age, a serious
> and respectable man. [...]
>
> Which turned out to be the whole mechanism.
>
> Because a ridiculous idea carried by a ridiculous man stays
> ridiculous. A ridiculous idea carried by a respectable man, every
> day, for thirty years, slowly stops being ridiculous. //
>
> The jeering thinned. Then it stopped. Then — quietly, one by one —
> other men started carrying them. [...]
>
> And for a while, Londoners didn't call them umbrellas.
>
> They called them **Hanways.**

---

### PERDE 6 — ZAFER · 7:00–8:00

> Jonas Hanway died in 1786. [...]
>
> Within a generation, the umbrella was ordinary. Within two, it was
> so thoroughly British that the rest of the world began to associate
> it with London — the black umbrella, the grey sky, the man walking
> to work beneath it. //
>
> A sun-shade, invented for deserts and designed for emperors, had
> been captured by the rainiest capital in Europe and converted into
> the national symbol of not complaining about the weather. [...]
>
> But here is the part worth keeping.
>
> Every object sitting in your house right now — the ordinary ones,
> the ones you don't think about — passed through a moment like this.
> Somebody was first. Somebody was laughed at. //
>
> And the reason you can use it without embarrassment today is that
> they decided the laughter was survivable. [...]
>
> The next time it rains, and you put up an umbrella without thinking
> about it at all —
>
> that is what thirty years of being shouted at bought you.
