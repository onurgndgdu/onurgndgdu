### PERDE 1 — KANCA · 0:00–0:25

> In the 1890s, doctors began warning women about a new medical condition. [...]
>
> It had a name. It had a list of symptoms. It appeared in medical journals
> and respectable magazines. Physicians described its progression and
> warned that the damage would become permanent. //
>
> It affected only women. And it only appeared when they rode bicycles.
>
> There was no such disease.

---

### PERDE 2 — MAKİNE · 0:25–2:00

> To understand why anyone needed to invent an illness, you have to
> understand what had just been invented. [...]
>
> For most of the century, a bicycle was a penny-farthing — an enormous
> front wheel with a seat perched above it. Dangerous, expensive, and
> essentially unrideable in a skirt. //
>
> Then, in the late 1880s, the safety bicycle arrived. Two equal wheels.
> A chain drive. Pneumatic tyres soon after. It was stable, it was cheap,
> and for the first time it could be ridden by almost anyone. [...]
>
> By the mid-1890s there was a cycling boom on both sides of the Atlantic.
> And a large share of the new riders were women. //
>
> It is difficult, now, to see what that meant.
>
> A respectable woman in 1890 did not travel alone. She was accompanied,
> or she was driven, or she did not go. Her movements were visible, and
> they were supervised. [...]
>
> The bicycle removed all of that in a single object. It was cheap enough
> to own privately. It needed no horse, no driver, no permission, and
> no companion. A woman could get on it and simply leave — and nobody
> would know where she had gone until she came back. //
>
> It also could not be ridden in the clothing women were expected to wear.
> Corsets restricted breathing. Long heavy skirts caught in the chain.
> Within a few years, women cycling in shortened skirts and bloomers were
> a common enough sight to be argued about in newspapers. [...]
>
> So the bicycle did not just move women around. It changed what they wore,
> where they went, and who knew about it.
>
> And then the medical warnings started.

---

### PERDE 3 — TEŞHİS · 2:00–3:30

> The phrase appears in print by 1895, in an American magazine describing
> a wearied and exhausted "bicycle face." [...]
>
> But it is a British physician who is usually remembered for it. In
> February 1897, a doctor named Arthur Shadwell published an article in
> the National Review titled "The Hidden Dangers of Cycling." In it, he
> claimed to be the first to diagnose the condition. //
>
> The description was specific, which is what made it persuasive.
>
> The face of the cyclist, it was said, became drawn and strained. The jaw
> clenched. The eyes grew hard and staring, fixed on the road ahead. The
> lips pressed thin. The whole expression settled into a mask of tension
> and effort. [...]
>
> And the crucial claim: that with enough repetition, the expression would
> not relax again. The face would set that way. Permanently. //
>
> Bicycle face did not arrive alone. The same period produced bicycle
> stoop, bicycle heart, bicycle hand, bicycle twitch — an entire family of
> conditions named after a machine. [...]
>
> Some of these described real strain. Anyone who has gripped handlebars
> too long has felt some of it. That is what made the rest of it work:
> the diagnosis was attached to something people genuinely experienced.
>
> It simply added a consequence that was not true.

---

### PERDE 4 — ASIL MESELE · 3:30–5:15

> Here is the detail that gives it away. [...]
>
> Men rode bicycles too. In enormous numbers — more than women did. They
> gripped the same handlebars, climbed the same hills, and arrived with
> the same red strained faces. //
>
> But the warnings were not addressed to them.
>
> The literature on bicycle face concerns itself almost entirely with
> women, and specifically with what it would do to their appearance. Not
> their health. Their faces. [...]
>
> And the surrounding arguments were not medical at all.
>
> The physician Arabella Kenealy argued that strenuous exercise drained
> something from women that they needed in order to bear children —
> that a woman who spent her vitality on sport was spending it on the
> wrong thing. //
>
> Others worried about where the bicycle allowed women to go. Unchaperoned.
> Out of sight. To meet whoever they liked. Clergymen and newspaper
> correspondents warned about what unsupervised young women might do with
> an afternoon and a machine that could carry them fifteen miles. [...]
>
> Read together, the complaints are not about faces, or hearts, or hands.
>
> They are about a woman being somewhere nobody chose for her.
>
> But that objection, said plainly, sounds like what it is. Said as
> medicine, it sounds like concern. //
>
> A father forbidding his daughter the bicycle because he disapproved
> of her independence is a tyrant. A father forbidding it because a
> physician has warned of permanent disfigurement is being careful.
>
> That is the work the diagnosis was doing. It converted disapproval
> into advice.

---

### PERDE 5 — ALDIRMAYANLAR · 5:15–6:45

> It did not work. [...]
>
> Women kept riding, in rising numbers, straight through the years the
> warnings were loudest. //
>
> And doctors pushed back. In 1897 — the same year as Shadwell's article —
> the Chicago physician Sarah Hackett Stevenson stated plainly that cycling
> was not injurious to any part of the anatomy, because it improved the
> general health.
>
> She was not offering a counter-theory. She was pointing out that there
> was nothing there. [...]
>
> This is the part that is easy to miss. Bicycle face was never a settled
> medical fact that was later overturned. It was contested at the time, by
> doctors, in public, while the panic was still running. //
>
> Some people simply chose to believe the frightening version.
>
> And in 1896, Susan B. Anthony — asked about the bicycle by the journalist
> Nellie Bly — said it had done more to emancipate women than anything else
> in the world. [...]
>
> She was not talking about exercise. She was talking about the thing
> everyone involved understood perfectly well, on both sides of the
> argument.
>
> The bicycle was not a health question. It never had been.

---

### PERDE 6 — SONRASI · 6:45–7:45

> Bicycle face disappeared without ever being formally disproved. [...]
>
> It did not need to be. The cycling boom continued, millions of women
> rode, and none of their faces froze. The diagnosis simply stopped being
> repeated, and then stopped being remembered. //
>
> Today it survives as a curiosity — a funny thing the Victorians believed,
> filed alongside their other oddities.
>
> But it was not a mistake. Nobody misread a study. There was no study.
> It was an objection to women's freedom, dressed in the one language that
> was hard to argue with. [...]
>
> And that is the part worth keeping.
>
> The bicycle is still sitting in garages, completely unremarkable. The
> fear attached to it has evaporated so completely that the warning now
> sounds absurd — which is exactly how it will always sound, afterwards. //
>
> It never sounds absurd at the time. At the time, it sounds like a doctor.
