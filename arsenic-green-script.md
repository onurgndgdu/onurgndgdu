### PERDE 1 — KANCA · 0:00–0:25

> In 1775, a Swedish chemist mixed copper with arsenic and produced a green
> that nobody in Europe had ever seen before. [...]
>
> Within fifty years it was on wallpaper, on dresses, on children's toys,
> on sweets, and in the paint on nursery walls. //
>
> Doctors spent half a century arguing that it was killing people.
>
> Britain never banned it.

---

### PERDE 2 — RENK · 0:25–2:00

> To understand why anyone would paper a nursery in arsenic, you have to
> understand how difficult green used to be. [...]
>
> For most of history there was no good green. Dyers made it by layering
> blue over yellow, and the result was dull, uneven, and faded badly.
> A strong, stable green was something money could not reliably buy. //
>
> Then, in 1775, Carl Wilhelm Scheele produced copper arsenite. It was
> brilliant. It was cheap. It did not fade. [...]
>
> Scheele knew what was in it, and it was sold anyway. It was named after
> him. Scheele's green. //
>
> In 1814 a brighter version arrived — copper acetoarsenite, sold as
> emerald green or Paris green. And this is the point where a chemical
> curiosity becomes a social fact. [...]
>
> The Victorians did not use this colour sparingly. It went into wallpaper
> by the mile, into dress fabric, into candles, toy paint and sweets. //
>
> Britain was, at the time, the largest arsenic producer in the world. Most
> of it came out of the ground as a by-product of copper mining, and for a
> long time nobody had known what to do with it.
>
> Now there was something to do with it.

---

### PERDE 3 — ÇİÇEKÇİ KIZLAR · 2:00–3:45

> The first people it killed were not the people who bought it. They were
> the people who made it. [...]
>
> Artificial flowers were a large London trade. Fabric leaves, wired and
> shaped by hand, then dusted with green powder to colour them. The work
> was done by young women, in small rooms, for long hours. //
>
> The powder got on their hands. It got into their food. They breathed it
> all day. [...]
>
> On the twentieth of November, 1861, a nineteen-year-old flower maker
> named Matilda Scheurer died in London.
>
> At the inquest her mother described what her daughter had been bringing
> up before she died. It was green. //
>
> Arsenic was found in her stomach. It was found in her liver. It was
> found in her lungs.
>
> The verdict was accidental death. [...]
>
> Punch did not accept that. A piece appeared shortly afterwards pointing
> out that when the same work had made the same people ill over and over
> again, calling the result an accident was a decision rather than a fact. //
>
> A chemist was commissioned to test the artificial foliage being sold in
> London shops. The figure reported from that work was that a single
> headdress carried enough arsenic to poison twenty people. [...]
>
> In 1862 Punch ran a cartoon called The Arsenic Waltz. Two skeletons,
> dressed for a ball.
>
> This part of the story is not disputed by anyone. The women who handled
> the pigment were being poisoned by it.
>
> What happened next is where it gets difficult.

---

### PERDE 4 — DUVARLAR · 3:45–5:25

> Because a much larger claim was being made at the same time. Not that
> the powder was dangerous to handle — but that the finished paper, hung
> on a wall, dry, untouched, could poison the family living in the room. [...]
>
> In April 1862, a doctor named Thomas Orton was called to a house in
> Limehouse, in east London. The Turner family. He was treating a
> three-year-old girl. Three of her brothers and sisters had already died. //
>
> The children had been diagnosed with diphtheria. The treatments for
> diphtheria had done nothing. [...]
>
> Orton looked at the room and noticed the wallpaper. It was green. He had
> it tested, and it was loaded with arsenic. He wrote the case up and sent
> it to the Lancet. //
>
> And then he ran into the problem that would keep this argument alive for
> the next forty years.
>
> Nobody was eating the wallpaper. [...]
>
> If the paper was poisoning the room, there had to be some way for arsenic
> fixed in a wall covering to reach a child across the room. Dust was
> suggested. But illness was reported in houses where the arsenical paper
> had already been papered over. //
>
> The answer seemed to arrive in 1893. An Italian physician, Bartolomeo
> Gosio, showed that mould growing on arsenical pigment released a
> volatile arsenic compound into the air. [...]
>
> In the 1930s the chemist Frederick Challenger identified it:
> trimethylarsine. A gas, produced by damp walls, in rooms papered green.
>
> It looked like the case was closed. [...]
>
> It was not.

---

### PERDE 5 — MORRIS · 5:25–7:10

> The most celebrated wallpaper designer in Britain was William Morris.
> His greens were very good. [...]
>
> They were good partly because they were arsenical, and the arsenic had
> a specific source.
>
> Devon Great Consols was a copper mine near Tavistock, opened in 1844 by
> a group of investors that included Morris's father. It made the family
> rich. And as the copper ran down, it became one of the largest arsenic
> producers in the world. //
>
> Morris held shares in it. He sat on its board. [...]
>
> Chemical analysis of his firm's early papers, published by the
> environmental scientist Andrew Meharg, found arsenical pigments in the
> majority of the first designs. Morris and Company appears to have
> stopped using them in the early 1870s. //
>
> Which makes what he wrote in 1885 difficult to read.
>
> In a letter to his friend Thomas Wardle, Morris dismissed the whole
> arsenic scare. The doctors, he wrote, had been bitten by witch fever.
> When a family fell ill, the fault was more likely in the drains than in
> the walls. [...]
>
> It is worth being precise about what is strange here. He was not
> defending a product he was still selling. He was defending the idea that
> it had never been dangerous — after quietly agreeing that it was. //
>
> There is a simple explanation, and it is not a flattering one. His
> customers had begun asking whether his wallpaper was safe. Removing the
> arsenic answered the customers. Denying the danger answered everything
> else — the mine, the fortune, the thirty years of work already hanging
> on other people's walls.

---

### PERDE 6 — NASIL BİTTİ · 7:10–8:10

> No law ever ended this. [...]
>
> The Arsenic Act of 1851 regulated the shop counter — who could buy
> arsenic and how it had to be coloured before sale. It did not touch
> manufacturers. In the 1880s campaigners drafted a bill for a general
> ban on arsenic in household goods. It went nowhere. //
>
> What happened instead was that people stopped buying green.
>
> Manufacturers began advertising their papers as arsenic-free, because
> that had become a selling point. By 1900 the Home Office could describe
> British wallpaper as practically free of arsenic — not because it had
> been prohibited, but because the market had quietly abandoned it. [...]
>
> And then, a century later, the explanation fell apart.
>
> In 2005 the chemists William Cullen and Ronald Bentley reviewed the
> gas theory and concluded that trimethylarsine is far less toxic than
> had been assumed — too weak to account for the deaths attributed to it.
> Some of those Victorian households may have been made ill by the damp
> and the mould rather than by the colour on the wall. //
>
> So the mechanism everyone finally agreed on was probably wrong.
>
> But Matilda Scheurer is still dead. The pigment did kill people — just
> not, perhaps, the ones in the parlour. [...]
>
> What this story records is a country that argued about a poison for fifty
> years, never banned it, got the science wrong, and got rid of it anyway.
>
> Not because it was proved. Because it stopped selling.
